=== EXAMPLE 1 (Industry Transparency) ===
When a product lists "fragrance" or "parfum" on the ingredient list, that single word can legally cover anywhere from a handful to several hundred individual chemical compounds. Most of them are never disclosed. I've explained why we leave it out of everything we make a few hundred times now, and the answer is mostly about that one word.

The disclosure gap is the first issue. The EU currently requires brands to declare 26 specific fragrance allergens above certain concentrations. That list has been expanded several times as more sensitising compounds were identified. The 26 on the list are not the only problematic ones. They are the ones where the evidence was strong enough to mandate disclosure.

The second issue is how sensitisation actually works, because it is not the same as an immediate reaction. The first ten times you use a fragranced product, you might notice nothing. Sensitisation builds with repeated exposure until the immune system has seen a compound often enough to respond - and once it does, the reaction tends to follow you across skincare, haircare and cleaning products, because the same compounds appear in all of them. It is also permanent.

I'm not saying fragranced products will sensitise everyone who uses them. Estimates for significant sensitisation sit somewhere between 1-3% of the general population, higher for people with existing skin conditions. That is not a majority. It is also not trivial, and the cost to the person who develops it is high.

The case for fragrance is aesthetic and commercial. Products that smell pleasant sell better, and that is a legitimate consideration. We made a different choice. Everything Skinstinct makes is fragrance-free, including the preservative system, the emollients and the carrier ingredients where scented variants are common. It makes the product less appealing on first use. Most customers who ask about it assume we left it out by accident.

If you have sensitive skin, or have reacted to a product before and never worked out why, ask the brand what their "fragrance" entry actually contains. Not us specifically - any brand. Most won't be able to tell you in full. That's useful information too.

=== EXAMPLE 2 (India-Specific Context) ===
In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns came from customers who mentioned the texture or feel of the product as the reason. When I mapped those returns against the customer's location, the pattern was immediate: 71% were from customers in cities with average annual humidity above 70%. Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Kolkata.

We were using a formulation originally developed for a climate it was not designed for.

Most skincare formulations in the Indian market are either imported directly or developed using ingredient systems and efficacy benchmarks from European and Korean markets. This is not inherently a problem - the actives science translates. But the vehicle, the base that carries the actives, is calibrated for a different climate. A moisturiser formulated for Oslo in February has different occlusive requirements than one for Chennai in August. The emollient-to-humectant ratio, the occlusive percentage, the overall texture that comes out of the formulation - these are downstream of climate assumptions that were made by the formulator for their market, not ours.

The specific issue in our case was that our first serum base was too film-forming for high-humidity skin conditions. In a dry climate, that film helps retain moisture. In Mumbai in July, the same film traps sweat under the product, changes the skin feel within 20 minutes of application, and produces exactly the greasy, heavy sensation our customers were describing. The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the wrong context.

We reformulated using a lighter base system with adjusted humectant-to-occlusive balance. Returns from humid cities dropped to 8% in the following quarter. The active ingredient concentrations stayed identical.

I bring this up not because our reformulation is a selling point - it should have been right the first time. I bring it up because the climate assumption problem is almost never discussed openly in Indian skincare. Brands import formulations or use contract manufacturers running formulations designed elsewhere, and the adaptation step gets skipped because it's expensive and because customers often can't articulate what's wrong beyond "it doesn't feel right." The texture data usually exists somewhere - in returns, in reviews, in customer service logs. The connection to climate and base formulation chemistry is rarely made.

If you're building a skincare brand for the Indian market, the first question to ask your contract manufacturer is what climate the base formulation was validated for. If the answer is anything other than "we'll run stability testing under Indian temperature and humidity conditions," that's worth investigating further.
